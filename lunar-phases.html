Why the Moon Has Phases

The Sun always lights exactly half of the Moon. As the Moon circles Earth every 29.5 days, we see a changing share of that sunlit half. Press play, drag the slider or click a phase to see how the Sun, Earth and Moon line up.

Key ideas

1Half is always lit

Like Earth, the Moon has a day side and a night side. The Sun always lights the half that faces it, whatever the phase.

2Phases are a viewing angle

The phase is the overlap between the sunlit half and the half facing Earth. At new Moon they don't overlap; at full Moon they match exactly.

3Not Earth's shadow

A common myth. Earth's shadow only reaches the Moon during a lunar eclipse, which can happen only at full Moon.

4Same face, always

The Moon spins once per orbit (tidal locking), so the red dot always points at Earth. We never see the far side from here, though it gets just as much sunlight.

529.5 days, not 27.3

The Moon circles Earth in 27.3 days relative to the stars. But Earth also moves around the Sun, so the Moon needs about 2 extra days to line up with the Sun again.

6No eclipse every month

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to Earth's orbit, so at most new and full Moons it passes just above or below the Sun–Earth line.

Key ideas

1Two kinds of month

The Moon makes one full turn against the stars in 27.3 days (the sidereal month). But by then Earth has moved about 27° around the Sun, so the Moon needs about 2.2 more days to be back between Earth and the Sun. New Moon to new Moon is 29.5 days (the synodic month). Try the “+ 27.3 days” button right after a new Moon.

2The real path never loops

The Moon is only 1/389 of the way to the Sun and moves around Earth at ~1 km/s, while Earth races around the Sun at ~30 km/s. So the Moon's true path around the Sun is a gently wavy circle that always bends toward the Sun. Loops appear here only when the Moon distance is exaggerated more than ~29×.

312.4 lunar months a year

365.25 ÷ 29.53 ≈ 12.37. Twelve lunar months make only 354 days, which is why purely lunar calendars drift ~11 days a year against the seasons, and why some years have 13 full Moons.

4Everything turns the same way

Seen from above the North Pole, Earth orbits the Sun, Earth spins, and the Moon orbits Earth all anticlockwise. That shared direction is why the Moon drifts east across the sky night after night.

5Same phases, new view

The close-up uses the same idea as the Phases tab: the Sun lights one half of the Moon. Here you also see that the direction to the Sun slowly rotates as Earth travels, and the phases follow that rotating direction, not the stars.

6Not to scale

At true scale Earth would be a speck 0.02 px wide at this size, and the Moon's orbit would be smaller than one pixel. Set Moon distance to ×1 to see how tightly the Moon really hugs Earth's path.

Scale note: in reality the Moon is about 30 Earth-diameters away and the Sun is ~390 times farther still, so sunlight arrives in effectively parallel rays, as drawn here.
